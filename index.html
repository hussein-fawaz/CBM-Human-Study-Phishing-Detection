Can people catch the model's mistakes when they see its concepts?

An online study for a concept bottleneck model. Each participant judges 2 real emails, before and after seeing an AI assessment. Three groups see the AI in three ways, and one of the two answers is deliberately wrong. The main question is whether showing the concepts behind a decision helps people notice and correct that error.

What is a concept bottleneck model?

A normal classifier maps an email straight to a label, and nobody can see why. A concept bottleneck model (CBM) has to pass through a middle layer of human-readable concepts: it first decides which warning signs are in the email, then computes the label only from those signs. Because nothing else reaches the label, every decision can be explained by the concepts it used, and a person can correct a concept and see the decision change.

x · the email. The raw text, the same input as any classifier.
c · the concepts. 10 yes/no warning signs predicted from the email, each with a quote as evidence. This is the "bottleneck".
y · the label. Phishing or legitimate, computed only from c, the signs that are present.

What we want to learn

People tend to follow an AI even when it is wrong, and explanations that read well can make this worse. A concept bottleneck model exposes checkable intermediate claims ("it asks you for a password: no"), so a person can disagree with one claim and see the verdict change. RQ2 tests whether that helps.

RQ1 · EFFECTIVENESS

Does seeing the AI improve accuracy?

Accuracy before vs after the AI on the email where the AI is right, in each arm.

RQ2 · AI ERRORS · PRIMARY

Do concepts help people catch AI mistakes?

Whether the participant still labels the planted error correctly after seeing the AI.

RQ3 · RELIANCE

Do people rely on the AI the right amount?

Over-reliance: right before the AI, wrong after it on the planted error. Under-reliance: disagreeing with a correct AI.

RQ4 · USABILITY & TRUST

Is it usable, and does trust change?

A 5-item usability scale (0–100), explanation quality, and trust and self-confidence before vs after.

RQ5 · WHO BENEFITS

Does it work for everyone?

Age (65+ vs younger), gender, technical background, education, past phishing victimisation, prior trust in AI.

RQ6 · CORRECTION IN PRACTICE

Do people use "I disagree", and does it work?

Concept arm only: how often people flip a concept, whether they flip the one that was wrong, and whether the recalculated verdict is then right.

Who takes part

  • Prolific, adults 18+, fluent in English, desktop or phone.
  • Age quota: 40% aged 65+ through Prolific's age filter (older adults are a heavily targeted group).
  • Gender: Prolific's balanced-sample option.
  • Arm assignment: separate study links per age stratum, each with balanced round-robin over the 3 arms, so age is balanced across arms. Within a session, the 2 emails are drawn from the pool and their order is random.
  • Pay: about £1.70 for ≤ 10 minutes (≈ £10/h).
  • Exclusions: failed attention check, unfinished session, or total time under one third of the median. Fixed before data collection.

How many participants

The primary outcome is one yes/no per person: did they still label the planted error correctly after seeing the AI? Set the expected rates and the sample size to see the power of each comparison (two-sided, α = 0.025 each because there are two comparisons).

Ethics and participant safety

  • Approval from the SUPSI ethics process before recruitment; informed consent on the first screen.
  • Links are never clickable and their paths are replaced by /xxxx; the consent page asks people not to type or guess them.
  • The emails are from the 2008 CEAS corpus; addresses in them are already anonymised. Some contain adult spam wording; the consent page says so.
  • Planned deception: the AI is wrong on purpose on 1 email. The debrief names it, explains why, and says the AI is a research prototype.
  • Responses are anonymous (Prolific ID only, deleted after payment); participants can withdraw at any time and their data is removed.
  • The debrief ends with 3 practical phishing tips, so taking part leaves people safer.
